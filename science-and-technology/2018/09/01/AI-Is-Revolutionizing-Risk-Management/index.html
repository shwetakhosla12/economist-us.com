AI Is Revolutionizing Risk Management

What industry practice and academic research agree on — and where they diverge — about deploying AI across the risk management lifecycle.

Sep 1st 2018 • By Shweta Khosla • The Economist

The bottom line, in plain English: industry and academia are converging on the same conclusion from different directions. KPMG's 2018 Future of Risk Survey shows risk leaders racing toward agentic AI that runs with minimal human oversight; a parallel academic study of practitioners finds the very same people most worried about data privacy, bias, and the loss of transparency that comes with it. The path forward isn't choosing one over the other — it's building governance sturdy enough to let the automation move fast.

Keywords — AI risk management, agentic AI, GRC, model governance, AI literacy, data privacy, algorithmic bias, guardrails

I. Introduction

Risk management is being reengineered, not just updated. That is the framing KPMG uses in its 2018 report on AI in risk management, and it captures something real: 400 executives surveyed for the firm's Future of Risk Survey ranked AI and generative AI as by far the most popular technologies for handling additional risk responsibilities over the next three to five years. Ninety-eight percent said digital acceleration — AI and advanced analytics — has already improved how their organizations identify, monitor, and mitigate risk.

At the same time, a 2017 Bryant University honors thesis by Lukas Ludwig, built on qualitative interviews with risk, IT, and academic professionals, found a workforce still wrestling with the basics: data privacy, algorithmic bias, and whether anyone actually trusts what the model outputs. Read together, the two sources describe the same transition from two different vantage points — one from the executive suite looking at technology roadmaps, the other from the practitioners who have to make the systems trustworthy day to day.

II. The Five Stages of AI Maturity

KPMG frames the industry's progress as five stages, moving from experimentation to full transformation:

Stage What It Looks Like
1. PrototypeExploring automation, ML, or AI in risk execution, but not yet in full production.
2. SiloedPoint solutions automate single activities; may or may not connect to the firm's GRC systems.
3. IntegratedConnected, end-to-end solutions tied to GRC, with a human in the loop for key decisions.
4. AgenticEnd-to-end solutions running key processes without requiring a human in the loop.
5. TransformativeAI-native methodologies fully replace traditional risk frameworks.

KPMG's own data suggests most organizations are still clustered in stages two and three — siloed point solutions cleaning up control inventories and flagging duplicate risks — even as leadership eyes stage four and five. Credit and fraud risk teams are the exception: they have used machine learning for anomaly detection and automated decisioning for decades, and their track record is now the reference case other risk functions point to when building the case for AI internally.

III. Where AI Delivers Value Across the Risk Lifecycle

The KPMG report maps distinct value across six stages of the risk lifecycle:

  • Risk identification. AI generates process flows, detects emerging risks, and recommends mappings to risk taxonomies and controls — catching risks earlier and more accurately than manual review.
  • Risk assessment. AI recommends risk ratings, generates and monitors key risk indicators, and calculates residual risk, making assessment more probabilistic and consistent.
  • Risk mitigation. AI supports response-strategy decisions and automates mitigation actions, from identifying root causes to monitoring alerts.
  • Risk monitoring. Continuous, real-time monitoring of risk indicators replaces point-in-time reporting.
  • Risk review and reporting. AI automates report generation, thematic analysis, and standardized control reporting.
  • Testing and validation. AI automates control testing and anomaly detection across large datasets, continuously learning from historical outcomes.

IV. The Human Side: What Practitioners Actually Worry About

Ludwig's thesis interviewed eight professionals across risk management, IT/AI development, and higher education, then coded their responses into seven recurring themes. The three most frequently raised — each cited by six of eight interviewees — were data security and privacy, trust and transparency, and the need for concrete AI safeguards. Bias and ethical concerns, AI literacy, and governance frameworks followed close behind, each mentioned by five interviewees.

“LLMs must be used with care and expertise to avoid nonsensical outputs.”
— Michael Howell, Protecht Group, as cited in Ludwig (2017)

The comparative analysis is where the study gets more interesting than a simple ranked list. Risk management professionals framed data risk in terms of legal and reputational consequences of a breach. IT and AI specialists took a more technical angle, pushing for enterprise-contained data collection and paid AI tool tiers with stronger privacy controls. Academic interviewees emphasized a simpler discipline: share only what's essential with any AI system, and be skeptical of chatbots handling anything sensitive at all. All three groups converged on one point — that guardrails, audits, and human oversight are not optional extras but the price of using AI safely.

V. By the Numbers: Quantifying the Shift

Both sources back their claims with figures worth putting side by side. On the industry side, KPMG's Future of Risk Survey (400 executives) found that 98% of respondents said AI and advanced analytics had already improved risk identification, monitoring, and mitigation — with AI and GenAI ranked, by a wide margin, as the most popular technology for absorbing additional risk responsibilities over the next three to five years.

On the practitioner side, Ludwig's interview data can be read the same way. Converting his raw theme counts (out of eight interviewees) into percentages makes the consensus — and the gaps — easier to compare directly:

Theme Interviewees Citing It Share of Sample
Data security & privacy6 of 875%
Trust in AI & transparency6 of 875%
AI safeguards (guardrails, audits, oversight)6 of 875%
AI literacy5 of 862.5%
Bias & ethical concerns5 of 862.5%
AI governance frameworks5 of 862.5%
AI in proactive risk identification3 of 837.5%

The pattern is informative on its own: three-quarters of interviewees, across every professional background represented, independently raised the same three concerns — privacy, trust, and safeguards — without prompting. Proactive risk identification, the theme closest to what executives are most excited about, was the least frequently mentioned by the people doing the implementation. That gap between leadership enthusiasm and practitioner caution is the same tempo mismatch noted in the next section.

It's also worth putting a number on what KPMG means when it says AI can “calculate residual risk.” The underlying formula is a standard one in risk management, predating AI entirely — what AI changes is how fast and how often it gets recalculated:

Residual Risk = Inherent Risk × (1 − Control Effectiveness)
Standard enterprise risk management formula

Inherent risk is the raw exposure before any controls are applied; control effectiveness is expressed as a percentage (0 to 1) representing how much of that exposure a given control removes. A process with $10 million in inherent risk and controls rated 80% effective carries $2 million in residual risk. Historically, control-effectiveness ratings were reassessed periodically — quarterly or annually — because the calculation was manual. What KPMG's report describes as AI “making risk assessment more probabilistic” is this same formula run continuously against live data, with control effectiveness itself modeled as a probability distribution rather than a single static estimate, and residual risk recalculated in real time as conditions change. The math hasn't changed; the frequency and precision of applying it has — which is exactly the shift from Stage 2 (siloed, point-in-time) to Stage 4 (agentic, continuous) in KPMG's own maturity model.

VI. Where Industry and Research Agree

Set the two sources side by side and three points of alignment stand out. First, both identify credit and fraud risk as the proving ground — the place AI earned regulatory comfort first, and the model other functions now follow. Second, both treat governance as a prerequisite, not an afterthought: KPMG frames it as embedding AI into GRC systems from the start; Ludwig's interviewees independently converged on governance frameworks, guardrails, audits, and human oversight as the load-bearing structure that makes everything else possible. Third, both flag the same tension — AI is simultaneously the accelerator and the constraint, capable of cutting costs and surfacing insight while creating exactly the kind of opacity and bias risk that risk teams exist to catch.

Where they diverge is tempo. KPMG's report is written from the vantage point of firms already planning for agentic AI operating with minimal human oversight — stage four of its own five-stage model. Ludwig's interviewees, closer to the daily mechanics of deployment, describe an organization still building the literacy and trust required to get comfortably through stage three. That gap between where leadership wants to go and where the workforce feels ready is, arguably, the real story: the technology may be ahead of the governance needed to run it safely.

VII. Getting Started: What Both Sources Recommend

The practical guidance converges more than it diverges. KPMG's roadmap starts with pinpointing pressure points — mapping the risk lifecycle to find where human effort and inconsistency are highest — then getting data governance in shape before piloting narrow, high-impact use cases and scaling only what works. Ludwig's recommendations, drawn from practitioner interviews rather than executive strategy, land on the same three pillars from a different angle: a governance framework with clear roles and policies, organization-wide AI literacy training so employees recognize when to override a model's output, and layered safeguards — guardrails, regular audits, and human oversight on high-stakes decisions.

Both sources also agree on sequencing: govern before you scale, and don't try to automate the whole risk function at once. KPMG's phrase for this is “pilot with purpose”; Ludwig's interviewees put it more bluntly — AI should fail internally, in testing, before it ever reaches an end user.

VIII. Conclusion

The direction of travel is not in question. AI is moving risk management from backward-looking, spreadsheet-driven assessment toward real-time, predictive, and increasingly autonomous operation. What both sources make clear is that the technology's value is inseparable from the discipline wrapped around it — the data governance, the audits, the literacy training, the human-in-the-loop design choices that keep speed from outrunning safety. Organizations that treat AI as a bolt-on will get bolt-on results. The ones pulling ahead, in both KPMG's telling and in the practitioners Ludwig interviewed, are the ones redesigning the operating model itself — keeping humans focused on judgment and oversight while AI absorbs the production work underneath it.
